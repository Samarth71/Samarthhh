Dr. Asati — AI Health Assistant ↗
2026

An AI-powered health assistant that helps users understand medical queries, symptoms, and general health information.

AIHealthWeb
The story behind it ↓

The Problem. People around me — family, neighbours — would either ignore symptoms or spend hours on Google, ending up more confused and scared than before. Smaller towns like Gondia don't always have easy access to doctors for every small query.

Why I built it. I wanted something that felt like asking a knowledgeable friend — not a medical textbook. I built Dr. Asati so that anyone, anywhere, could get basic health information without needing an appointment or a medical degree to understand the response.

What it does for you. Dr. Asati helps you understand what your symptoms might mean, what questions to ask your doctor, and whether something needs urgent attention. It doesn't replace doctors — it helps you show up to them better informed.

Why AI will change everything
And why most people aren't ready
March 2026 · 4 min read
↓

I've been thinking about this a lot lately. Everyone around me is talking about AI like it's just another tool — like the calculator, or the internet. Something that makes things faster. But I think they're missing the point completely.

AI isn't just making things faster. It's changing who gets to do things at all. A few years ago, if you wanted to build a health app, you needed a team of doctors, engineers, designers, maybe some investment. Today, I built Dr. Asati — an AI health assistant — mostly by myself, at 14, from Gondia. That's not a small thing. That's a fundamental shift in who gets to build.

Most people aren't ready because they're still thinking in the old model. They think the value is in knowing things — degrees, credentials, years of experience. But AI can know things too. The real value is going to be in judgment. In understanding what problem actually matters. In caring enough to solve it the right way.

The people who are going to fall behind aren't the ones who don't use AI. They're the ones who use it but don't think. Who let it replace their thinking instead of amplifying it. That's the trap.

I don't know exactly what the world looks like in ten years. Nobody does. But I know that the people building right now — even imperfectly, even slowly — are going to understand it better than anyone who just watched from the side. So I'm building.

Building as a teenager
What shipping real products teaches you
February 2026 · 3 min read
↓

Nobody tells you how weird it feels to ship something real for the first time. You've been working on it for weeks, maybe months. You've fixed the same bug three times. You've rewritten the whole thing once because you hated how it looked. And then one day you just... put it out there. And people use it.

That happened to me with Dr. Asati. I built it because I genuinely wanted something like it to exist — a simple way to understand health stuff without googling for an hour and ending up more confused. I didn't know if anyone else would care. Turns out some people did.

Here's what building taught me that nothing else has: the gap between "I think I know how to do this" and "I actually did it" is enormous. You don't see that gap in tutorials. Tutorials always work. Real projects don't. They break in weird ways, at weird times, for reasons you didn't expect. And the only way through is just sitting with it until you figure it out.

People ask me how I learned to code. I tell them I just started building things. That's genuinely it. Not courses, not certifications. Just: have a problem, try to solve it, fail a lot, eventually have something that works.

I'm not saying this to sound impressive. I'm saying it because I think a lot of people are waiting for the right time, or the right amount of knowledge, before they start. There is no right time. Start now. Build something bad. It'll get better.

On Innovation
Ideas are cheap. Execution is everything.
January 2026 · 1 min read
↓

Everyone has ideas. Seriously, everyone. The person sitting next to you on the bus has an idea for an app. Your relatives at dinner have ideas for businesses. Ideas are not the scarce thing. They never were.

What's scarce is the willingness to actually do something about it. To sit down, open your laptop, and start — knowing it's going to be messy and slow and probably wrong at first. Most people never get there.

Innovation, real innovation, is just execution repeated enough times that something new exists in the world. That's it. It's not a flash of genius. It's showing up and doing the work even when the work is frustrating and the results aren't there yet.

I've started thinking of innovation less like a talent and more like a habit. You do it enough times and it gets easier. The first time I built something from scratch it took forever. Now I move faster, not because I'm smarter, but because I've done it before and I know the shape of the process.

So if you have an idea — just start. The idea will change a hundred times once you actually touch it. That's fine. That's the whole point.
