Dr. Asati — AI Health Assistant ↗
2026

An AI-powered health assistant that helps users understand medical queries, symptoms, and general health information.

AIHealthWeb
Visit Website ↗
The story behind it↓

The Problem. People around me — family, neighbours, relatives in smaller towns — would either ignore symptoms for weeks or spend hours searching Google, only to come out more anxious and confused than before. They weren't looking for a diagnosis. They just wanted someone to help them make sense of what they were feeling and tell them whether it was serious enough to see a doctor.

Why I built it. I've watched family members dismiss symptoms they should have acted on, and panic over ones that were completely harmless — simply because they had no way to get a calm, informed second opinion without booking an appointment. I wanted to build something that felt less like a search engine and more like asking a knowledgeable friend — one who speaks plainly, doesn't overwhelm you with medical jargon, and knows when to say "go see a doctor today" versus "this sounds fine, monitor it."

How it works. Dr. Asati is powered by a large language model fine-tuned with health-focused prompting. Every response is deliberately kept conversational — no bullet-point walls of text, no cold clinical language. I spent a lot of time on the prompt design to make sure it never overstates certainty, never dismisses symptoms without reason, and always reminds users that it's a first step, not a final answer.

What it does for you. Describe your symptoms in plain language and Dr. Asati will help you understand what they might indicate, what questions to bring to your doctor, what warning signs to watch for, and whether something needs attention today or can wait. It won't replace a doctor — but it'll make sure you walk into that appointment informed instead of scared.

What I learned. Building in the health space forces you to think hard about responsibility. Every design choice — how confident the tone sounds, what words you use, when to escalate — carries real weight. It made me a more careful engineer.

Megmee — Anonymous Messaging App ↗
2026

A messaging app where you can chat with anyone without adding a phone number. End-to-end encrypted, simple, and open to all.

WebMessagingPrivacyE2E Encrypted
Visit Website ↗
The story behind it↓

The Problem. Every major messaging app today treats your phone number as the price of entry. WhatsApp, Telegram, Signal — all of them ask for it upfront. That's fine if you want a permanent contact. But sometimes you just want to have a quick, private conversation with someone without swapping personal details or creating an account that lives forever.

Why I built it. I kept running into situations — online communities, new acquaintances, collaborations — where I wanted to message someone without the friction of "add me on WhatsApp." I wanted a tool that was genuinely zero-friction: no sign-up, no download, no phone number. Just open a link and talk. So I built one.

How it works. Megmee uses Firebase Firestore for real-time message sync and implements end-to-end encryption so messages are encrypted on your device before they ever reach the server. The server never sees your plaintext. I built live presence indicators so you can see when someone is typing, and designed the UI to feel as clean and instant as a native app — despite running entirely in the browser.

What you get. Create a room, share the link, start chatting. End-to-end encrypted, real-time, no account required, no phone number, no install. Works on any device with a browser. When you're done, close the tab — no trace left.

What building it taught me. Megmee was the project where real-time architecture finally clicked for me. I had to think hard about data modelling for live sync, keeping Firestore costs near zero on the free tier, and how to design security rules that actually hold up. It taught me more about how the web works under the hood than any course ever could.

Eranatis — AI Writing Tool ↗
2026–

An intelligent writing assistant designed to help users write better, faster — without losing their voice.

AIWritingWeb
Visit Website ↗
The story behind it↓

The Problem. Most people have something worth saying — they just struggle to say it well. The existing landscape gives you two bad options: tools like ChatGPT that write the whole thing for you (and it sounds like ChatGPT, not you), or grammar checkers that fix typos but leave your ideas untouched. There's almost nothing that sits in the middle — something that sharpens your thinking, helps you structure ideas, and makes your writing sound like the best version of you.

Why I'm building it. I noticed that the best writing advice — "cut this sentence," "this paragraph buries your main point," "this word is doing no work" — rarely comes from tools. It comes from good editors and thoughtful readers. Eranatis is my attempt to make that kind of feedback available to anyone, not just people with access to editors. I want it to feel like a collaborator, not a ghostwriter.

How I'm building it. The core challenge is preserving voice. I've spent significant time on the prompting layer — making sure Eranatis suggests and questions rather than overwrites. The interface is deliberately minimal: a clean editor where AI suggestions appear as options, not automatic replacements. You stay in control of every word.

What it will do for you. Whether you're writing a cold email, a college essay, a blog post, or a business proposal — Eranatis will help you move from a rough idea to a finished piece faster, flag where your argument loses momentum, suggest stronger word choices, and tighten your structure. The goal: your ideas, expressed better.

Where it's at. Eranatis is actively in development. The core writing experience is live — I'm currently working on deeper context awareness and personalisation features that adapt to your individual writing style over time.

BillGuard India — Medical Bill Auditor ↗
2026

An tool that audits medical bills against national fair-pricing benchmarks and generates formal dispute letters and phone scripts to fight hospital overcharges in India.

WebHealthFinanceIndia
Visit Website ↗
The story behind it↓

The Problem. Medical bills in India are notoriously opaque. Hospitals routinely charge several times the fair rate for procedures, and most patients pay without question — either because they don't know what the fair price is, or because they don't feel empowered to push back. The information asymmetry is enormous: a well-connected patient or healthcare advocate knows exactly what to demand. Everyone else just pays.

Why I built it. This hit close to home. Watching family members get handed hospital bills with no way to verify whether the charges were legitimate — and no idea what recourse they had — made me angry. The data to fight these overcharges exists publicly (CGHS rates, state health scheme benchmarks) but it's buried in government PDFs that nobody knows about. I wanted to take that data, make it searchable, and give people the exact words to use when they dispute a bill.

How it works. I built a pricing database from CGHS (Central Government Health Scheme) rates and mapped them across city tiers, since costs in Mumbai are different from costs in a Tier-2 city. The audit engine uses fuzzy procedure matching — so "knee replacement" and "total knee arthroplasty" resolve to the same benchmark — and flags any line item that exceeds the fair-price threshold for your location. Gemini then takes the flagged items and generates a formal dispute letter and a phone script tailored to your specific bill, ready to send or read out.

What it does for you. Upload or paste your bill, select your city tier, and BillGuard will tell you which procedures were overcharged, by how much, and what the benchmark price is. Then it hands you a ready-to-send dispute letter and a phone script — so you can fight back immediately, with evidence, in language that hospitals take seriously.

What I learned. The hardest part wasn't the AI — it was the data. Building a reliable pricing database from inconsistent government PDFs, then writing a fuzzy matcher good enough to handle the chaos of real-world medical billing terminology, took far longer than expected. It taught me that for civic tech, the data layer is everything.

ScamSentry — Email & Message Fraud Checker ↗
2026

A free, browser-based security tool that scans emails, messages, and links for signs of phishing and fraud. Runs entirely on-device — no data ever leaves your browser. Returns a 0–100 trust score with plain-language explanations.

SecurityPrivacyBrowserFraud Detection
Visit Website ↗
The story behind it↓

The Problem. Scam messages are getting frighteningly good. The days of obvious "Nigerian prince" emails are long gone — modern phishing copies real brand emails pixel-for-pixel, spoofs legitimate-looking domains, and uses psychological pressure ("your account will be suspended in 24 hours") to bypass your better judgment. Most people have no quick, private way to check a suspicious message before clicking a link or typing in a password.

Why I built it. Every existing tool I found either required you to create an account, upload your message to a server, or wait for a slow scan. None of that is acceptable when you're holding a message that might contain your bank credentials. I wanted something that runs entirely in your browser — instant, private, zero data leaving your device — that could give a plain-language verdict in seconds.

How it works. ScamSentry runs a layered heuristic analysis entirely on your device. It checks sender email addresses for lookalike domains (e.g. "paypa1.com" vs "paypal.com"), flags free email providers impersonating brands, scans message text for urgency patterns ("act now", "verify immediately"), threats, generic greetings, and explicit requests for passwords or OTPs. For links, it checks for raw IP addresses, missing HTTPS, redirect chains via shorteners, and domain age signals. Each signal is weighted and combined into a 0–100 trust score with a plain-language explanation of exactly what triggered it.

What it does for you. Paste a suspicious email, message, or link — or drag in a .txt, .eml, .msg, or .html file — and ScamSentry gives you a trust score, a risk verdict (Safe / Suspicious / Likely Scam), and a breakdown of every red flag it found. No account, no sign-up, no data ever sent anywhere. Works on any device with a browser.

What I learned. Building a heuristic engine is humbling. You quickly realise that clever scams will always slip through pattern-matching — which is why I was careful to frame ScamSentry as a "helpful first check," not a security guarantee. Getting the trust score calibrated so it doesn't cry wolf on legitimate emails — but still catches real threats — required a lot of tuning and real-world test cases. The UX lesson: when dealing with security tools, plain language and honest limitations build more trust than false confidence.

Why AI will change everything
And why most people aren't ready
March 2026 · 4 min read
↓

I've been thinking about this a lot lately. Everyone around me is talking about AI like it's just another tool — like the calculator, or the internet. Something that makes things faster. But I think they're missing the point completely.

AI isn't just making things faster. It's changing who gets to do things at all. A few years ago, if you wanted to build a health app, you needed a team of doctors, engineers, designers, maybe some investment. Today, I built Dr. Asati — an AI health assistant — mostly by myself.

Most people aren't ready because they're still thinking in the old model. They think the value is in knowing things — degrees, credentials, years of experience. But AI can know things too. The real value is going to be in judgment.

The people who are going to fall behind aren't the ones who don't use AI. They're the ones who use it but don't think. Who let it replace their thinking instead of amplifying it. That's the trap.

Building as a developer
What shipping real products teaches you
February 2026 · 3 min read
↓

Nobody tells you how weird it feels to ship something real for the first time. You've been working on it for weeks, maybe months. You've fixed the same bug three times. And then one day you just... put it out there. And people use it.

Here's what building taught me that nothing else has: the gap between "I think I know how to do this" and "I actually did it" is enormous. You don't see that gap in tutorials. Tutorials always work. Real projects don't.

I'm not saying this to sound impressive. I'm saying it because I think a lot of people are waiting for the right time, or the right amount of knowledge, before they start. There is no right time. Start now. Build something bad. It'll get better.

On Innovation
Ideas are cheap. Execution is everything.
January 2026 · 1 min read
↓

Everyone has ideas. Seriously, everyone. The person sitting next to you on the bus has an idea for an app. Ideas are not the scarce thing. They never were.

What's scarce is the willingness to actually do something about it. Innovation, real innovation, is just execution repeated enough times that something new exists in the world.

I've started thinking of innovation less like a talent and more like a habit. You do it enough times and it gets easier. So if you have an idea — just start.
